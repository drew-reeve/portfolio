Water as a Human Right: Conflict Over a Basic Necessity

Should water be a common good or a market commodity? And what does a "right to water" ask of the state?

The questions

  1. What role has the state played in providing, or limiting, its citizens' access to water, and what institutions define and protect water rights?
  2. Should access be driven by market mechanisms, or provided as a common good to everyone in a state?
  3. How does international law treat a human right to water, and how is water access tied to poverty and underdevelopment?

The argument

Water isn't a clean fit for the "global public good" label. As Lyla Mehta puts it, access is mediated by power relations and highly unequal, so it's governed locally. Even so, the international community has increasingly recognized a right to water, often through commentary on other rights rather than binding treaty text.

States must provide access to water in a way that both benefits their citizens and allows a functioning market.

The paper's answer is a middle path: state planning and incentives that curb waste in industry and agriculture, local education about water use, and a stronger presence for international organizations and NGOs to make sure access is adequate and efficient. The cost of getting it wrong falls on the poor, through health costs and a weaker working population that keeps a state stuck in the poverty cycle.

Where it led

The tension at the heart of this paper, between full cost recovery and access for people who can't pay, is still the one I find most interesting. Living in the West, where water law, snowpack and recreation economies collide, has only made it feel more concrete.
