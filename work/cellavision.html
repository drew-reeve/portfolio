Standardizing the Hematology Stain Workflow with Aerospray® and CellaVision®

Does an automated cell classifier still get it right when you change how the slide was stained? We tested eight variables to find out.

When
Published March 2021
Where
ELITechGroup, Logan, Utah
Authors
Abraham Verdoes, Ryan Cowley, Andrew Reeve
My role
Co-author and research analyst: data analysis and results
The question Study design Results What I did White paper (PDF) ↗

The question

Clinical labs depend on consistency. A blood smear is stained, then a digital analyzer like the CellaVision DM1200 photographs it and classifies the white blood cells. If the stain varies, the classifier can drift, and a lab technician has to correct more of its calls by hand.

The Aerospray Hematology Pro lets labs adjust stain settings. Our objective was to verify that white blood cells on Aerospray-stained slides are classified correctly by the CellaVision across a range of staining settings and options, using a clear bar: at least 95% agreement with an experienced laboratory technician.

Example white-blood-cell images from the study.

Study design

We prepared 124 blood smears by hand using the standard wedge method from Wintrobe's Clinical Hematology, loaded them in 12- and 30-slide carousels, and stained them in batches under two programs (Rapid and Wright-Giemsa) and two buffers (pH 6.8 and 7.2). Holding everything else at the default, we varied one setting at a time:

  1. Fixation
  2. Intensity
  3. Red/blue ratio
  4. Spin
  5. End rinse
  6. Carousel size
  7. Partial vs. full carousel
  8. Fixative type

Each condition was then run through the CellaVision and scored against the technician's classification. A condition passed if agreement was 95% or higher.

Results

The default programs met the 95% bar across carousel sizes, partial and full loads, and both fixatives, under both buffers. Most low, default and high settings passed too; a handful of extreme settings fell short, which gave labs a clear, documented operating range rather than a single "safe" recipe.

Why it mattered

The paper gave customers evidence that they could tune their stains to local preference without giving up automated classification accuracy. That's a sales claim turned into a testable hypothesis, with a pass/fail criterion set before the data came in.

What I did

As the research analyst on the team, I organized and analyzed the classification data, built the results tables that show each setting against the 95% criterion, and co-wrote the paper. It's a small study, but it's a good example of how I like to work: define the question and the bar for success first, then let the data decide.
