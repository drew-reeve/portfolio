The World's Struggle for Clean Water

Roughly one in six people alive in 2008 lacked adequate access to water. My MIAGE capstone asked who is positioned to change that, and how.

The question

The National Academy of Engineering named access to clean water one of its Grand Challenges for the 21st century, noting that a lack of clean water is responsible for more deaths than war. The capstone starts there and asks how governments, non-governmental organizations and businesses can each help expand access to safe drinking water, and why the issue deserves a place at the top of future global leaders' agendas.

The argument

  • "Peak water." Drawing on Gleick and Palaniappan, the paper frames freshwater as a resource with natural limits. Unlike oil, it has no substitute.
  • Measurement first. WHO's UN-GLAAS assessment found that a scarcity of information on national water and sanitation policy, financing and staffing was itself a major barrier to progress.
  • Low-cost technology. Solar disinfection (SODIS), which uses only sunlight and discarded PET bottles, shows how simple, local methods can reach the poorest households. The paper also notes its limits: cloudy seasons, small volumes and water that stays murky.
  • Shared responsibility. Business, government and NGOs each have a role, and progress on goals like the Millennium Development Goals depends on leaders treating water as a priority, not a footnote.

Where it led

Reading this now, I can see the start of the questions I care about most: who controls water, who pays for it, and whether poor communities can use it to climb out of poverty. Today I'd come at it with data, for example measuring how access affects household income or child health across Andean communities, rather than through policy review alone.
